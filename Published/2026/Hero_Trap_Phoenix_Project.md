# The Hero Trap

The most dangerous bottleneck in your sprint isn't a low performer.

It's your best engineer trying to help.

Mid-sprint, management dropped a regulatory emergency on us. Vague requirements, a deadline nobody believed, and zero room to mess up.

Our Tech Lead did the obvious thing. He grabbed the whole workload because he didn't think the team could handle it.

Three days in, he'd isolated himself completely, coding late into the night. In *The Phoenix Project*, Brent became a bottleneck because the system forced him there. Nobody forced our Tech Lead. He volunteered. That makes it harder to spot and harder to fix.

While he was buried in solo code:
- PRs sat unreviewed.
- Architecture questions went unanswered.
- Blockers piled up and sprint flow stalled.

He wasn't just working late. The whole sprint was stuck waiting on one person.

I sat down with him over coffee.

"I'm shielding the team," he told me. "They aren't ready for this level of complexity under deadline."

"I get it, you're protecting them," I said. "But if you keep building this solo, what happens next time?"

"They still won't be ready," he admitted.

"Exactly. If you never let them touch high-stakes code alongside you, they never will be. You aren't protecting the team. You're capping their growth."

To be clear: sometimes a senior engineer needs to go heads-down and ship. That's not the trap.

The trap is when emergency mode becomes the default gear.

That one conversation didn't fix anything. Next high-pressure sprint, he was right back to solo coding. But at least he caught himself doing it and pulled in a pair before going too deep.

## Three questions before the next urgent request

1. If your best engineer took 2 weeks off tomorrow, would the sprint survive?
2. When the last urgent item landed, did the senior volunteer to solo it, or was the team genuinely unable to take it?
3. Does your Definition of Ready require a pairing partner for complex items?
