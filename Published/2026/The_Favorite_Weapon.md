### Psychology Stack

- **Status Quo Bias:** We cling to familiar retro formats because changing feels risky.
- **Commitment Escalation:** The more sprints we run the same retro, the harder it is to admit it stopped working.
- **Pluralistic Ignorance:** Everyone thinks they're the only one who's bored with the retro format. Anonymous input breaks the illusion.

## 📝 Phase 2: Polished Draft — LinkedIn Post

```
For five sprints, I ran the same retrospective. Open discussion, same three columns. Every session, the same two or three people talked. The rest sat quietly.

Over ten people in the room. I was hearing from three. It took me five sprints to see that the problem wasn't the team. It was the format.

So I changed the weapon.

The team handled medical underwriting, so I built the retro around an operating theatre. Problems became internal bleeding, wins became vital signs, improvements became the treatment plan.

I asked if they wanted to try it or keep the open conversation. They hesitated, but chose to try.

The theme gave them a reason to say yes. The real change was underneath. I set Miro to private mode so each person filled in their notes on a timer without seeing anyone else's. No anchoring, no groupthink.

When the board opened, the quiet ones weren't quiet at all. They'd written about quality gaps, missing impact analysis, friction they'd never said out loud. The same things two or three people had been raising alone for months. I grouped the notes: seven people had written the same thing.

They didn't suddenly start speaking up. But one vocal member said it was the best retro we'd had. I agreed, because it was the first time I actually heard from everyone.

Miyamoto Musashi wrote: "Do not have a favourite weapon. To become over-familiar with one weapon is as much a fault as not knowing it sufficiently well."

The open-discussion retro was my favourite weapon. Familiar, comfortable, cutting in one direction. The team didn't need a louder invitation to speak. They needed a format that didn't require speaking at all.

Next retro, try this: before anyone speaks, set a five-minute silent writing timer. Private input, no names. Then open it all at once. Count how many themes repeat.
```
