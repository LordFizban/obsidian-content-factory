# Respect Is Not Being Nice

**Publish Date:** Tuesday, March 10, 2026
**Pillar:** Manager Partnership / Psychological Safety bridge
**Format:** Text Post
**Reference:** Mark Graban's "Respect for People" (Lean)

---

## 🧠 Phase 1: Agile Coach Roast

> **The Roast:**
> This story has teeth. You didn't coddle the PO — you asked two direct questions in front of the team, and the answers exposed the gap. That's not "being mean." That's Transparency (Scrum Pillar #1). The PO was shifting blame to an external vendor when the root cause was his own coordination failure. If you'd stayed silent, you'd have modeled a dysfunction: "It's okay to blame the outside when you haven't done the work inside."
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Scrum Value: Respect.** The 2020 Scrum Guide says the team must "respect each other to be capable, independent people." Respect means trusting someone is *capable* — and calling it out when they're not acting like it.
> - **Lean Principle: Respect for People.** Toyota's interpretation is NOT "be polite." It's "challenge people to grow." Mark Graban's work on this is definitive — respect means holding the standard, not lowering it.
> - **Agile Principle #4:** "Business people and developers must work together daily." The PO was not working *with* the vendor — he was sending emails into a void.
>
> **The Framework:**
> **Warrior vs. Statesman.** You were the Warrior in the room — you confronted the issue directly. But you switched to Statesman in the 1-on-1 afterward. That's the maturity most SMs lack. The Warrior wins the moment; the Statesman preserves the relationship.
>
> **The Upgrade:**
> The story is strong. One risk: LinkedIn readers might read "I called him out in front of the team" as aggressive. Frame it as *two questions, not an accusation*. The power is in the questions — you didn't lecture, you asked. Let the reader feel the silence after the PO said "no" the second time. That's where the tension lives.

## 📄 Phase 2: Draft Post (Editor-in-Chief Reviewed)

```
He said the vendor was blocking him.

During refinement, the PO told the team he was stuck.
"The vendor isn't giving me what I need."

I asked: "Have you actually spoken to them on the phone?"

No.

"Did you talk to them on the phone before this became a blocker?"

No.

I felt the room shift. He'd been emailing the vendor for days. No call. No alignment. Just messages disappearing into a thread no one read.

I didn't push further in that moment.

Later, one-on-one, I told him:
"Two days without a response means the channel isn't working. Pick up the phone."

Some people would say I should have handled it privately from the start.

But here's why I didn't:

He was telling the team the vendor was the problem.
If I stayed silent, the team would believe it.
The real problem wasn't the vendor. It was coordination.
And the team needed to see that distinction — in real time.

Mark Graban writes about "Respect for People" in Lean.
It doesn't mean "be nice."
It means "challenge people to grow."

Respect is believing someone is capable of more — and saying so.

What I did wasn't comfortable.
But comfort wasn't what he needed.
Clarity was.

When was the last time you challenged someone not to punish them — but because you believed they could do better?
```
