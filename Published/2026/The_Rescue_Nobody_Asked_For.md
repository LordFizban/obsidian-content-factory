### Psychology Stack

- **Intention-Behavior Gap:** You intend to collaborate. Under stress, you default to control.
- **Fundamental Attribution Error (self-directed):** You attribute your jumping in to "helping." Others attribute it to "not trusting us."
- **Invisible Cost:** Nobody complains when you do their work. The cost is in what they never learn.

## 📝 Phase 2: Polished Draft — LinkedIn Post

```
The vendor was feeding our procurement team vague promises. We were buying a new business process management tool, and every answer was "we can customize that" without a single technical detail.

I asked to be copied on the email chain. I told myself I just wanted visibility. Within a week I was writing analysis notes on every vendor response, sending my viewpoint to procurement before they had time to form their own.

I kept a private channel with the procurement lead. I never corrected her in front of the vendor. I thought that made it respectful. But the message was the same either way: I don't trust you to evaluate this without me.

This was years ago, in a project management role. But the pattern followed me.

As a Scrum Master, I still do it. To this day, when I am stressed, I cut my PO mid-sentence because I sense he is about to say something incorrect to a stakeholder. I catch myself drafting talking points he never asked for. The impulse is the same one I had with procurement: waiting feels irresponsible.

Esther Derby writes that there is territory between micro-management and turning a team loose. I wasn't navigating that territory. I was skipping straight to the side that made me feel useful.

The procurement team never complained. Nobody does when you do their work for them. But they never got the chance to learn vendor management on their own terms. I saved time on one contract and probably cost them confidence on every contract after.

Next time you feel the urge to jump in, ask yourself one question: is this a crisis, or is this discomfort?
```
