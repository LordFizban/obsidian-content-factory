## 📝 Phase 2: Polished Draft — LinkedIn Post

```
My team delivered 50% more story points than planned. Nobody was celebrating.

During a daily standup, a developer said it in front of everyone: "Sprint planning is useless. We end up doing whatever is asked anyway." Several people nodded. The Product Owner said everything would get better. It sounded like consoling a child. I looked around. Nobody believed her.

I wasn't listening for frustration. I was listening for what the system was telling me.

User stories were arriving after refinement, sometimes the day before planning. Acceptance criteria were vague or missing. The boundary between planned and unplanned work had dissolved.

In The Phoenix Project, Gene Kim names four types of work. When I mapped our sprint against them, barely half was planned business work. The rest was changes, internal IT tasks, and unplanned work nobody had approved.

I went to the IT Director. I didn't argue the team was overloaded. I showed that the quality of incoming demands was the constraint. I pinpointed one gap: "acceptance criteria." Our scrum teams understood it. Business stakeholders had never heard the term.

We organized a one-day training for key business stakeholders with external agile coaches. The goal wasn't to teach them Scrum. It was to teach them their responsibilities when bringing work to the team.

The next sprint wasn't perfect. But for the first time in months, the team delivered close to what they'd planned. The sprint became plannable again.

Next Monday, try this: look at every ticket your team worked on last sprint. Separate them into four categories — planned business work, internal IT work, change requests, and unplanned work. Count the percentages. In my experience, if unplanned work exceeds 20%, your sprint isn't slow. It's being eaten.
```
