# The Inner Citadel

## LinkedIn Post Body

```
My team had just survived a sprint where half the work was unplanned.

We were still catching our breath when the Teams message landed.

Two sprints earlier, executive management had flagged a strategic shift during Big Room Planning. But warnings and reality are different animals. The reality was four words from the Product Owner: "New product. Launch ASAP."

No requirements, no acceptance criteria, no timeline beyond "soon." Just four lines in a chat window and a team already stretched thin.

The standup felt like a pressure cooker. One developer said what everyone was thinking: "So we just drop everything again?" Another asked how you plan for something without a spec. The frustration wasn't about the product. It was about feeling like a leaf in someone else's wind.

I let them talk. For about five minutes the room was just venting from weeks of accumulated pressure.

Then I asked one question.

"What do we actually know right now, and what don't we know?"

Silence. Then someone pulled up the message and read it out loud. They started listing facts: a new product is coming, it's a priority, it came from BRP.

Then the unknowns. We don't know the scope. We don't know the technical stack. We don't know how it affects current commitments. We don't know stakeholders. We don't know the real deadline.

Three items on the left, nine on the right.

The panic wasn't about the product. It was about the story the team was telling itself: that everything was collapsing, that their work didn't matter, that leadership didn't care. Once we stripped the narrative and looked at raw facts, the picture changed. Three knowns and nine questions — but questions have answers.

The team decided to protect the current sprint. The new product would enter next planning with proper refinement, not get shoved in because someone sent a message. Leadership accepted the deferral but added parallel discovery. The outcome wasn't clean. Real outcomes rarely are. But the communication gap became a retro topic that outlived the sprint.

What mattered: the team stopped spiraling and started working. The composure came from the question, not from me. When you separate what you know from what you're afraid of, the fear loses most of its weight.

Marcus Aurelius wrote in Meditations: "Say nothing more to yourself than what first appearances report." Someone spoke four lines in a chat. That was the fact. Everything else was narrative.

Next time an external disruption hits your team, try this before the standup ends. Draw two columns on a whiteboard. Left column: what we know. Right column: what we don't know. Count them. I've never seen a team that didn't calm down once the unknowns had names.
```
