## 🧠 Phase 1: Agile Coach Roast

> **The Roast:**
> Three publishable layers identified. The post is NOT about people leaving. It's about the **system you built to integrate the new Tech Lead** — specifically, engineering "easy wins" by delegating real technical blockers so the new TL earns social credit before needing authority.
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Tuckman's Stages:** When you lose PO + TL + 2 devs and gain replacements, the team regresses from Performing to Forming. Most SMs know this. Almost none plan for it deliberately.
> - **Scrum Guide (2020):** "Causing the removal of impediments." Social integration *is* impediment removal — a new TL without credibility is an impediment.
> - **Lean: Respect for People.** Giving someone easy wins before hard accountability = respecting their need to build credibility.
>
> **The Framework:**
> **Lighthouse Leadership.** When half the crew changes, the lighthouse doesn't move. SM = the constant.
>
> **Anonymization Rules:**
> 🔴 Never hint you pushed the PO/TL changes. Frame as organizational transitions.
> 🔴 Never characterize old PO or old TL. No "uncoachable," no "passive."
> ✅ The tech lead onboarding system is fully safe and publishable.

## 📄 Phase 2b: Draft Post

```
In three sprints, half my team's key roles changed.

New developers. A new Product Owner on the horizon. And someone stepping into a technical leadership role for the first time.

If you've ever built a team over months — calibrating, trust-building, finally hitting a rhythm — you know what this feels like.

It feels like starting over. Except you don't get to start over once. You get hit again before you've recovered from the last one.

Most Scrum Masters react to this by running extra alignment sessions. Documenting everything. Trying to preserve what was built.

I didn't do that.

Instead, I focused on one person: the one taking on the technical leadership role.

Not because the others didn't matter. But because a team without a dependable technical anchor will struggle through everything else that's coming.

So I didn't start by briefing him on processes and ceremonies.

I started by giving him a problem.

Not a big, strategic problem. A real, concrete, annoying technical blocker the team had been stuck on.

He solved it in three days.

The team noticed.

Then I gave him another one.

By the end of the second week, developers were going to him directly. Not because I told them to. Because he had already helped them.

That's not onboarding. That's building dependability.

My goal wasn't to give him a title. It was to make him the person the team turns to when they're stuck. The title follows. The trust has to come first.

When your team goes through a major transition, don't try to preserve the old team.

Build the conditions for the new one to form faster.

What's the first thing you do when a key role changes on your team?
```
