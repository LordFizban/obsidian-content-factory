## 🧠 Phase 1: Agile Coach Roast

> **The Roast:**
> This isn't a story about a missing PO. This is a story about an organization that built a cross-functional team of Scrum Masters to lead an Agile transformation—and then let its own rituals die from scheduling conflicts. The PO (who is also the SMs' line manager) skipped the Retro. Sprint Planning got canceled because a few SMs had a 15-minute overlap. When the SM (you) tried to reschedule, the PO said "just go ahead without me." That's not flexibility. That's the old system reasserting itself through the back door. And the hierarchy makes it worse: speaking up against your own manager in a team session takes real Courage.
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Agile Principle #12:** "At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective." Regular means non-negotiable.
> - **Scrum Guide (2020):** The Sprint Retrospective requires the full Scrum Team. Skipping it is an organizational anti-pattern.
> - **Scrum Value: Courage.** The SM calling out the pattern—in the Retro, in front of the team—is the most Courageous thing in this story.
>
> **The Framework:**
> **Warrior vs. Statesman.** You tried the Statesman first (offered to reschedule). When that was rejected, you switched to the Warrior in the session itself: calling out the pattern of canceled rituals and naming the systemic hypocrisy — directly in front of a team whose PO is also your line manager. That's not just courage; it's principled risk-taking. The next move—taking it to senior leadership—is the Statesman's escalation: fixing the system, not just the symptom.
>
> **The Upgrade:**
> The punch line isn't "the PO skipped the Retro." The punch line is the *pattern*: Planning canceled for 15-minute overlaps + PO skipping Retros + the org calling this "Agile." That's what makes it a LinkedIn-grade insight. It's not one incident; it's a narrative shift. The PO-as-manager detail adds a power dynamic that makes the SM's stand even more notable — but be strategic about how much hierarchy you expose publicly.

## 📄 Phase 2: Draft Post (Second Pass — Editor-in-Chief Reviewed)

```
The fastest way to kill an Agile transformation isn't through resistance.
It's through "schedule conflicts."

I saw this first-hand.

Our organization formed a dedicated team of Scrum Masters to drive Agile transformation. Strong mandate, senior sponsorship, the works.

The team had recently been restructured. We were only a few sprints in — still forming, still calibrating. And then the Sprint Retrospective arrived.

The Product Owner and one team member notified us they couldn't attend. A traditional project steering meeting took priority. I offered to postpone. The PO's response: "I don't have another slot. Just go ahead without me."

So we did.

And the feedback in that retro was raw. One team member gave direct feedback about the PO — feedback that belonged in the room with the PO present. They mentioned they'd also raised it 1:1. But the signal was clear: the inspect-and-adapt loop was already fractured.

Then I spoke up.

A few days earlier, a Sprint Planning session had been canceled entirely. The reason? A couple of Scrum Masters had a 15-minute overlap with another meeting.

Fifteen minutes.

That was enough to cancel the entire session.

I told the team: this is the elephant in the room.

Canceling rituals sends a message. It tells every person in the room that these events are, in fact, optional. And with that, the organization is telling us something louder than any kickoff presentation ever could: agility is not the priority here. The traditional way of working still wins.

A PO who skips the Retro is not a scheduling accident. It's a signal. A Planning session canceled for a 15-minute overlap is not flexibility. It's a pattern. And that pattern tells the team exactly where Agile sits in the corporate hierarchy — below the steering meeting, below the status update, below the "real" work.

That's not a transformation. That's a performance.

My suggestion to the team was clear: take this to senior leadership. Because the problem isn't a missing PO or a scheduling conflict. The problem is an organization that hasn't yet decided if it actually values the system it claims to be building.

If your rituals are optional, your agility is a myth.

Have you seen rituals become "optional" in your organization?
What did you do about it?
```
