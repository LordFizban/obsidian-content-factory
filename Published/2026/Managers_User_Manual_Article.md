# What Your Manager Needs From You (That Nobody Taught You)

Your manager makes 50 decisions a sprint.

Three of them shape your team's next quarter.

Do you know which three?

---

I manage projects and serve teams as a Scrum Master at the same time.

As a project manager, I sit in every meeting with management. I coordinate. I report. I align. But when I wear the Scrum Master hat, that instinct disappears. The SM role doesn't relate to the manager the same way.

And that's the gap nobody talks about.

I have two perspectives on the same manager. Only one of them is talking to her.

Most Scrum Masters focus on the team and skip the manager relationship entirely. Sprint planning, retrospectives, impediments. That's correct. But it leaves a blind spot: the manager still makes decisions about your team. You just stopped being in the room when they happen.

Over the past two years, I've found three questions that close this gap. They're simple. They changed the way I work with management.

## "What decisions are you making about us this month?"

This question makes the invisible visible.

Early on, I noticed my manager making decisions that seemed unnecessary. Straightforward work was being designated as formal projects. Extra documentation was required for things the team could handle in a sprint.

I thought she was creating overhead.

I was wrong.

When I finally asked what was driving these decisions, I learned about an organizational review she was managing. The documentation wasn't bureaucracy. It was a requirement she was shielding us from.

My judgment was wrong because I didn't have her context.

That's the thing about managers. They operate in a layer you can't see from the team level. Budget reviews. Headcount discussions. Organizational restructuring. If you don't ask, you'll misread their decisions. And misreading your manager is expensive.

One question. Five minutes. It changed how I interpreted every decision she made after that.

## "What do you need from me that you're not getting?"

This question flips the relationship.

Scrum Masters are taught to serve the team. Nobody teaches you to serve the manager. But the manager is part of the system your team operates in. Ignoring her doesn't make her disappear. It just means you're managing half the system.

When I started regular one-on-ones with my manager, her questions were about delivery.

"Are there any risks to the team's deliverables during this sprint?"

That was the question. Every time. Deliverables. Deadlines. Output.

I answered those questions. But I also started bringing something else to the table: how the team was doing. Not just what they were delivering, but how they were working. Refinement quality. Collaboration patterns. Energy levels.

Months later, something shifted.

Her questions changed.

"How is the team? How is the refinement process?"

She stopped asking about deliverables first. She started asking about people first.

I didn't teach her this. I didn't lecture her about Agile. I just kept showing her a different lens, sprint after sprint, until she started looking through it on her own.

That's what the scrum master and manager relationship looks like when it works. Not the SM reporting to the manager. Not the SM avoiding the manager. The SM investing in the manager until the manager starts asking better questions.

## "What signals would tell you we're succeeding?"

This question aligns what "done" actually means.

Last week, I wrote about a vendor dependency that cost us five days of tester capacity. But the story didn't end there.

After that event, I sat down with the Business Owner to discuss what had happened. The team had marked a sprint item as complete. Tests were done on time. From the team's perspective, they had delivered.

From the Business Owner's perspective, they had not.

Her definition of success wasn't "tests completed." It was "test results positive." The team had finished the work. But because of the vendor dependency, the results weren't what she needed. In her eyes, it wasn't done.

Same sprint item. Two different definitions of success. Nobody had checked.

We agreed to take this to the Product Owner together. She committed to participating more in acceptance criteria definition going forward. Not because I told her to. Because she saw the gap herself when we talked about what "success" meant to her.

That conversation wouldn't have happened if I hadn't asked the question.

## What changed

These three conversations didn't fix everything. But they turned a reporting relationship into a partnership.

The first question gave me context I was missing. The second question shaped how my manager thinks about our team. The third question aligned what "done" means before the sprint starts, not after it ends.

Your manager isn't the obstacle. They're the person with the most context you're not using.

What's the one question you wish you'd asked your manager sooner?
