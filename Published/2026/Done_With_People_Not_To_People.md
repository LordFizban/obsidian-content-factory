# Done With People, Not To People

**Publish Date:** Thursday, March 12, 2026
**Pillar:** Psychological Safety
**Format:** Text Post
**Reference:** Mark Graban's Lean principle — improvement done *with* people, not *to* them.

---

## 🧠 Phase 1: Agile Coach Roast

> **The Roast:**
> This is the most relatable Scrum Master story you can tell. Management told the team to do what they were already doing — but nobody listened because the room was on fire. That's what happens when improvement is mandated instead of co-created. The team was already splitting capacity. Management "solved" the same problem and thought they fixed it. Classic top-down illusion of control.
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Scrum Guide 2020:** "Scrum Teams are cross-functional, meaning the members have all the skills necessary to create value each Sprint. They are also self-managing, meaning they internally decide who does what, when, and how."
> - **Lean Principle:** "Respect for People" means trusting the team closest to the problem to design the solution. Management mandating a structure violates this.
> - **Agile Principle #11:** "The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams."
> - **IKEA Effect (Behavioral Science):** People value what they helped build. The triage system worked because the team *owned* the experiment.
>
> **The Framework:**
> **Daily Kaizen.** The triage wasn't a revolution — it was a structured, repeatable improvement cycle. PO prioritizes, tech lead digs into root cause, devs rotate through defects. Small, owned, daily. That's Kaizen.
>
> **The Upgrade:**
> The story has a perfect ironic twist: management told you to do what you were already doing. That's the hook. Don't bury it — lead with it. Also: the post should NOT shame management. The point is that even the right answer fails when it's mandated. The *same solution*, co-created, succeeds.

## 📄 Phase 2: Draft Post (Editor-in-Chief Reviewed)

```
Management pulled us into a meeting to fix our defect problem.

They told us exactly what we were already doing.

Two years ago, my team was drowning in defects. Incoming was faster than outgoing. We were doing everything we could, but the flood wouldn't stop.

Management's answer: split the team. A few developers on defects. The rest on new features.

That was already our process.

We pointed it out. But tensions were high. It wasn't heard.

So instead of arguing, we ran an experiment.

I proposed a defect triage system:
— The PO decides priority: fix now or delay.
— The tech lead investigates root cause and creates a follow-up task.
— The sprint's defect developers attend triage, pull work, and close incidents.

No one mandated roles. The team chose who rotates in.
No one dictated priorities. The PO owned the call.
No one prescribed the fix. The tech lead traced the root.

We ran it for three sprints.

The difference wasn't the structure. The structure was similar to what management suggested.

The difference was ownership.

When the team designs the process, they protect it.
When someone else mandates it, they tolerate it.

Mark Graban calls this the core of Lean:
Improvement done *with* people, not *to* them.

The best process is the one your team built themselves.

What's one improvement that only worked because the team owned it?
```
