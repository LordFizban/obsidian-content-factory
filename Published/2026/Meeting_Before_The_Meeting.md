## 🧠 Phase 1: Spark & Roast (Agile Coach + Marketing Psychology)

> **The Roast:**
> This is a sleeper topic that every Scrum Master has experienced but almost nobody writes about. The meeting before the meeting is where Sprint Reviews actually succeed or fail. Most POs walk into Sprint Review cold — they haven't talked to the team since the last standup, they haven't seen the demo, and they don't know what the stakeholders are going to ask.
>
> The SM who fixes this isn't doing it in the ceremony. They're doing it on Monday or Tuesday — pulling the PO aside for 15 minutes to walk through what's shipping, what's not, and what the stakeholders care about right now.
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Scrum Guide (2020):** The Sprint Review is "a working session and the Scrum Team should avoid limiting it to a presentation." But if the PO is surprised by what's in the demo, it instantly becomes a presentation — because the PO goes into defensive mode.
> - **Warrior vs. Statesman:** The Warrior runs a great Sprint Review — polished demo, stakeholder management, on-time. The Statesman prepares the PO so thoroughly that the SM barely needs to speak during the ceremony.
> - **Strategic Gaze (Musashi):** Sight sees the Sprint Review. Perception sees the conversation between the PO and the stakeholders — and prepares for it in advance.
>
> **The Upgrade:**
> The story needs a specific sprint moment. When was the last time you walked a PO through the demo before Sprint Review and something changed because of that prep? That's the moment. The tension is: what would have happened if you hadn't done the prep?

## 📄 Phase 2b: Draft Post

```
The Sprint Review went perfectly.

Because the real meeting happened two days earlier.

On Wednesday, I pulled our PO aside. She was busy tweaking Jira statuses.

"Walk me through what you're showing on Friday."

She looked at me like I was asking a trick question.

"The demo is ready. The team knows what to show."

"I know," I said. "But do you know what the stakeholders are going to ask? And more importantly... are you going to talk about what *didn't* go well?"

Silence.

We spent 15 minutes going through three things:

1. What's shipping — and the actual business value of each increment.
2. What *didn't* ship — and how to courageously own our faults.
3. The one value-based question a stakeholder is most likely to ask.

On Friday, the Review was calm.

She didn't just show features; she explained the value. When it came to a delayed integration, she didn't flinch or hide it. She owned the gap, provided the reason, and laid out the next step. 

It takes courage for a PO to admit what didn't work. The alternative is pure theater — an unrealistic showcase that breaks trust.

That shift didn't happen because of a great demo.
It happened because of a 15-minute conversation on Wednesday.

Most Scrum Masters prepare the Sprint Review.
The ones making a real difference prepare the Product Owner.

The ceremony is the stage.
The prep is the rehearsal.
And nobody sees the rehearsal.

What does your Sprint Review prep look like — and who are you preparing?
```
