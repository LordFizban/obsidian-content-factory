# The Analyst's Notebook

## Post Body

"I understood almost nothing in my first technical meeting."

That's not my story. It's the opening line of an article written by an analyst on one of my teams.

Most people would bury that kind of confession. Merve turned it into the most honest piece about Business Analysis I've read this year.

She kept the notebook from that first meeting. Back then, she filled it with words she didn't understand: Postman, Token, JSON, Request, SQL. She compared it to her first English class in elementary school, where the teacher talked and she just wrote down words she couldn't translate.

Five years later, the same notebook looks completely different. Now her notes start with questions like: Why is this development requested? What's the real need? Who will use it? What are the error scenarios?

That shift matters more than it sounds. I've sat in Sprint Reviews where developers walk through API endpoints and stakeholders silently check their phones. The room isn't disengaged because the work is boring. It's disengaged because nobody is translating the work into something that matters to them.

Merve does that translation. In one Sprint Review, instead of walking through a list of acceptance criteria, she showed the room a single flow: what a customer sees when they hit an error, what they expected to see, and why the gap matters. The conversation shifted from "is it done?" to "is it right?" in under two minutes.

There's a line in her article that stuck with me: "Business Analyst olmak yaptigim is degil. Dunyaya bakis seklim oldu." ("Being a BA is not what I do. It became how I see the world.")

When someone describes their craft as an identity rather than a task, you're looking at someone who will keep getting better, with or without a development plan.

Reading her article made me think about something I don't do often enough. I notice growth in my team every day, in sprint reviews, in the way someone frames a question differently than they did six months ago, in the small moments that never make it into a retrospective. But I rarely stop and tell them what I actually see.

Merve's notebook reminded me that growth is easier to keep going when someone mirrors it back to you. Sometimes the most valuable thing you can do for your team isn't facilitating or removing blockers. It's simply saying: "I see how far you've come, and here's exactly what I noticed."
