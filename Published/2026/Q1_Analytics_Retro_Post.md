## 🧠 Phase 1: Agile Coach Roast

> **The Roast:**
> This is the most "practice what you preach" post you've ever considered. You coach teams on empiricism — inspect, adapt, measure, adjust. Now you're showing you do it with your own LinkedIn. That's integrity. But here's the trap: **don't make it about LinkedIn.** Your audience is Agile practitioners, not content creators. The hook must be about the Agile principle, not the platform.
>
> **The Canon:**
>
> - **Scrum Pillar: Inspection.** "Scrum Team members inspect Scrum artifacts and progress toward the Sprint Goal to detect undesirable variances." You're inspecting your *own* content artifacts. Same principle, different domain.
> - **Agile Principle #12:** "At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective." Your Q1 analytics review IS a retrospective — just for a team of one.
> - **Evidence-Based Management (Scrum.org):** EBM uses Key Value Areas (Current Value, Unrealized Value, Ability to Innovate, Time to Market) to guide decisions. You're doing exactly this: measuring current performance, identifying unrealized audience segments, testing innovation (Turkish experiment), optimizing time-to-engagement (Tue+Thu cadence).
>
> **The Framework:**
> **Daily Kaizen.** You've written about Kaizen before (W7, W8). This post is the meta-Kaizen: Kaizen *about* your Kaizen practice. It closes the loop. As you wrote in the Kaizen Bridge Post: "Progress over perfection." Now you're showing the progress with numbers.
>
> **The Upgrade:**
> Keep only 2-3 data points. More than that becomes a report, not a post. The reader needs:
> 1. One insight that surprised you (something you believed that was wrong)
> 2. One thing you changed based on data (the adaptation)
> 3. One thing you're still figuring out (the vulnerability)
>
> **Anonymization:**
> ✅ Sharing your own analytics is fully safe — it's your data, your platform.
> ⚠️ Don't share exact follower count or growth if you want to avoid seeming "small." Focus on relative changes (%, trends) rather than absolute numbers.

```
I run my LinkedIn the way I run a Sprint Retrospective.

Every quarter, I inspect the data. What worked. What flopped. What I got completely wrong.

Here's what Q1 taught me.

I published 25 pieces of content in 13 weeks. Some hit. Some didn't. One got over 650 impressions. Another barely broke 50.

The difference wasn't effort. It was the same amount of thought in both.

The difference was this:

Stories outperformed frameworks. Every single time.

When I told a specific story — a real moment from a sprint, a conversation that went sideways, a decision I had to make — people read it. When I shared a principle or a mental model without grounding it in a moment, they scrolled past.

Volume is a trap. 

I assumed posting 4 times a week would double my reach. It actually lowered it. Sticking to a strict two-post weekly cadence generated higher total reach than flooding the feed. Consistent pacing beats panic posting.

The third thing I learned is harder to admit.

Views don't automatically convert to followers. 

My organic follower conversion from these 25 pieces of content this quarter was essentially zero. The few followers I did gain came from 1:1 outbound networking, not people reading my work. The top-of-funnel reach looks good, but the conversion engine is flat.

None of this is surprising if you're an Agile practitioner.

Inspect. Learn. Adapt.

We coach our teams to do this every two weeks. But how many of us do it with our own work?

I now review my content data the same way I review sprint metrics. Not to optimize for likes. But to understand what actually resonates with the people I'm trying to reach.

What's one assumption about your own work that data proved wrong this quarter?
```

## 📐 Phase 3a: Marketing Psychology Analysis

| Principle | Application |
|:---|:---|
| **Pratfall Effect** | Admitting "what flopped" makes the author more relatable and trustworthy |
| **IKEA Effect** | Showing the retrospective process makes the reader want to do their own Q1 review |
| **Authority Bias** | Evidence-Based Management (Scrum.org) gives intellectual weight |
| **Contrast Effect** | High-performing posts vs. flops — the gap makes the insight vivid |
| **Commitment & Consistency** | "We coach teams to do this every two weeks. But how many of us do it with our own work?" — challenges the reader's consistency |
| **Loss Aversion** | "If you don't measure it, you can't improve it" — fear of invisible failure |
